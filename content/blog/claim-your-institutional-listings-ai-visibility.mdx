For the last decade, the answer to "where should a contractor be listed" has been a list of companies that sell listings. Yelp, Angi, BBB, HomeAdvisor, Houzz, Thumbtack. All of them have a sales team, and all of them have been very effective at convincing trades businesses that visibility is something you buy.

Our tracking data has been pointing somewhere else for three months.

Between July and September 2026 we ran 2,100 automated AI visibility checks against one Dallas–Fort Worth plumbing market and stored every source trail. [The headline finding](/research/the-directory-collapse-ai-citation-shift) was that commercial directory citations fell by roughly half. The finding underneath it is more useful to act on: **the fastest-rising source domain in the entire dataset was a small city's government website.**

*The split is not between big sites and small ones. It is between records that are sold and records that are kept.*

That framing is the useful one. An engine assembling an answer it will be held to has a strong reason to prefer a source with no commercial stake in the outcome. A licensing board does not benefit from your being recommended. A city business directory does not upsell. The information is boring, maintained by someone whose job is accuracy, and hard to manipulate — which from a verification standpoint is the whole value proposition.

The rest of this is the work order.

<h2 id="step-1">Step 1 — Inventory what already exists about you</h2>

You are not creating these records. Most of them already exist and have existed for years, which means they may already be wrong. Run these six searches and write down what you find.

| # | Search | What you are checking |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Your state license board's lookup, by license number **and** by name | Status, expiry, responsible party, exact entity name |
| 2 | `site:yourstate.gov "Your Business Name"` | Comptroller, secretary of state, registry records |
| 3 | `site:yourcity.gov "Your Business Name"` | Municipal business directory, permits, vendor lists |
| 4 | `site:yourcounty.gov "Your Business Name"` | County registrations, DBA filings |
| 5 | Your chamber of commerce's member directory | Listing presence, category, contact details |
| 6 | `"Your Business Name" license` (no site restriction) | Aggregators that have republished your license data |

For each, record five fields exactly as they appear: **business name, street address, phone, license number, status.** Not approximately. Character for character, including whether it says "LLC" or "L.L.C.", "Street" or "St."

Most contractors doing this for the first time find between one and three records that disagree with their website. That disagreement is the problem — not the listing's absence.

<h2 id="step-2">Step 2 — Fix the name first, everything else second</h2>

The most common and most damaging error in institutional records is a business name mismatch.

It happens for ordinary reasons. The legal entity is *Vincent Plumbing Holdings LLC* because that is what the lawyer filed. The license is in the responsible party's personal name because that is how the trade works. The website says *Vincent Plumbing* because that is what customers call it. The chamber listing says *Vincent Plumbing & Drain* because someone filled in a form in 2019.

A human reconciles those instantly. A verification system treats them as four claims that may or may not describe the same business, and confidence drops accordingly. This is the mechanism behind [the local entity gap](/research/local-entity-gap-ai-search), and it is the highest-leverage unglamorous fix available to a contractor.

Pick one canonical trading name. Make every record you control match it exactly. Where a legal name must differ — and on a license it often must — make sure the trading name appears on the same record as a DBA or alternate name, so the two are linked in one place rather than floating separately.

*The whole ladder is an afternoon. It is an afternoon almost nobody spends.*

**The license board (30 minutes).** For Texas plumbers, the [Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners](https://tsbpe.texas.gov/); for most other trades, the [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/). Confirm current status, correct responsible party, correct entity name, and an address you still occupy. If anything is wrong, the board has a change form and it is usually quick. This single record carried more citation weight than Yelp, Angi, and BBB combined by September.

**The city (1 hour).** Many municipalities run a business directory, a registered-contractor list, or a permits portal that exposes contractor names publicly. Find yours, confirm you are on it, and correct the details. If your city has no directory but issues trade permits, your name is likely already in a public permits database — make sure it is attached to the right entity name.

**The state registry (30 minutes).** Comptroller or secretary of state, depending on the state. This is the record that establishes the entity exists and is in good standing. Franchise tax status lapsing is a common and invisible problem; it makes the registry record say something unhelpful about you.

**The chamber (1 hour).** Correct the listing if you are a member. Do not buy an upgrade for AI reasons — the listing's existence and accuracy is what gets cited, not its tier.

**County and DBA (30 minutes).** Lowest weight, but it is where assumed-name filings live, and those are what link your trading name to your legal entity in a public record.

<h2 id="step-4">Step 4 — Close the loop with your own site</h2>

Institutional records are corroboration. They corroborate something, and that something is your website, which remained the single most-cited domain about this business in every month we measured.

So the last step is to make the two agree in public. Put your license number on your site, in text rather than inside an image. Name the licensing body. State the entity name that appears on the license if it differs from your trading name. List the cities you actually serve rather than a county-wide claim you cannot support. [If a crawler cannot reach or parse any of that](/research/is-your-site-blocking-ai-crawlers), the corroboration has nothing to corroborate.

Then stop. Re-check in six months, or immediately after any change of address, phone, entity, or responsible party. These records fail silently — nothing emails you when a municipal directory gets rebuilt and drops your row.

<h2 id="what-this-is-not">What this does not do</h2>

Claiming institutional listings will not make a thin website rank. It will not manufacture reviews you have not earned, and it will not put you ahead of a better-documented competitor. The effect it has is narrower and more specific: it raises the confidence an AI system has that your business is real, licensed, located where you say, and called what you say it is called.

That is not a growth tactic. It is a precondition. The contractors getting named in AI answers are, on our data, the ones who clear it — and most of their competitors have left an afternoon of free work undone.
