# Your Safety Program Lives in Nine Places

Ask a contractor whether they have a safety program and the answer is yes. Ask where it is, and the honest answer is a list:

Manuals. Binders. PDFs. Email threads. SharePoint. A training platform. Manufacturer documents. Regulator websites. And the ninth place — the deepest and most fragile — the memory of your most experienced superintendent, [one of the fifth of the workforce now over 55](https://constructioncitizen.com/blog/41-percent-construction-workforce-retiring-2031-industry-needs-get-moving-c3/1802011).

[Last week's post](/research/the-ai-assisted-safety-system) argued that AI's honest role in safety is retrieval, documentation, and escalation. This one is the build guide: how nine places become one — and why the defining property of the system is not intelligence but *control*.

<h2 id="why-controlled-is-the-word">Why "controlled" is the word doing all the work</h2>

The naive version of this project is: dump everything into an AI tool, let crews ask questions. That version is worse than the binder, for a reason worth being precise about.

A general-purpose model answers from everything: your 2019 procedure and your 2024 revision, your state's rule and another state's, the manufacturer's manual and a forum thread — all delivered with identical confidence. Fragmentation at least *looked* unreliable. An uncontrolled assistant launders folklore into fluent, cited-sounding prose. For hazard questions, that is not a quality problem; it is a liability engine.

A controlled assistant inverts the design. It may answer **only** from an approved library, and every answer carries its papers:

| Every answer must include | Example |
|---|---|
| Source document | Company Trenching & Excavation Procedure |
| Revision / effective date | Rev. 6, effective March 2026 |
| Applicable jurisdiction | Federal OSHA + state plan, if applicable |
| Exact section | §4.2, Protective Systems |
| Human-approval flag | "Competent person must verify on site" |

And one more property, the one that makes it trustworthy: **when the library does not contain the answer, the system says so and escalates to the competent person.** A safety assistant is defined by what it refuses to improvise.

<h2 id="the-build">The build, in order</h2>

1. **Inventory the nine places.** Collect every safety artifact into one staging pile: program, procedures, training records, certs, toolbox talks, manufacturer instructions, applicable standards.
2. **Adjudicate versions.** For every document, one question: is this current? Superseded revisions get archived out of the answer path — this single step retires years of folklore.
3. **Tag what remains.** Source, revision date, jurisdiction, work types it applies to. Tedious, mechanical, and exactly the [preparation work AI now does cheaply](/research/construction-data-thrown-away) with human spot-checks.
4. **Wire the escalation path.** Unresolved questions route to a named person, and the routing is logged. The log itself becomes safety data: what crews keep asking is a map of where the program is unclear.
5. **Put intake on rails.** New revisions enter through one door — reviewed, tagged, replacing their predecessors — or the library rots back into a tenth place.

Notice the sequence is 80 percent librarianship and 20 percent AI. That ratio is the point. The intelligence layer is a commodity; the controlled library is the asset, and it is yours.

<h2 id="the-eleven-year-question">The eleven-year question</h2>

NCCER estimates it takes [about eleven years to train a craft professional to full equivalence](https://www.nccer.org/newsroom/how-apprenticeships-empower-adult-learners-and-bridge-the-construction-workforce-gap/) with the veterans now heading toward retirement. Nothing about a document library replaces that. But every hazard judgment your best superintendent has ever written down, corrected in a toolbox talk, or flagged in a near-miss report is either captured in the controlled library — with his name on the revision — or it retires with him.

The safety case for this system is the strongest one. It is also a rehearsal: the same architecture — controlled sources, versioning, provenance, escalation — is exactly what the next two posts apply to a domain with less blood and just as much money: specifications, codes, and the question of which document governs.
