A fixed-price contract is a prediction. You are telling an owner what a building will cost using prices you do not yet have, for material you will not buy for months, in a market where the single largest input category has moved more than 20% in a year.

Most contractors know this. What a surprising number have not done is put a number on the bet.

<h2 id="the-spread">The blended number is the one that gets people hurt</h2>

If you track a single construction cost index, you probably saw something around **8.9%** for nonresidential input prices over the year to August 2026 and planned accordingly. That number is accurate and it is nearly useless for a metal-intensive scope, because it blends categories that moved 20-plus percent with categories that barely moved at all.

*An escalation allowance built on the blended index covers roughly a third of what the metal actually did.*

For an electrician, copper is not a line item among many — it is the job. For a structural steel sub, steel mill products are the job. For a mechanical contractor, copper and aluminum together can carry half the material cost. Escalating those scopes at 8.9% is not conservative estimating. It is a 12-to-18-point shortfall dressed up as diligence.

<h2 id="why-it-persists">Why the exposure is still open</h2>

Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum rose from 25% to 50% in June 2025, and copper products carry the same 50% rate. Derivative metal products and some equipment categories sit lower. When the Supreme Court struck down tariffs issued under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act in February 2026, a lot of contractors read the headline and assumed the pressure was off. The ruling left Section 232 standing. The metals exposure survived the decision that was supposed to end it.

Meanwhile the contract language did not move. Public filings in the sector are blunt about where the risk sits: fixed-price and lump-sum agreements may not permit recovery of tariff-driven cost increases. That is a disclosure written for investors, and it describes exactly what happens on a $4M job when the steel package comes in 20% over the number you signed.

Three things keep the exposure open on jobs that should know better:

**The quote validity window is customary, not calculated.** Thirty days is a habit from a period when thirty days of metal price movement was noise. At current volatility it is a material position you are taking for free.

**Escalation clauses get traded away early.** They are the first thing an owner's counsel strikes, and the easiest thing to concede when you want the job. Conceding it is a decision with a price; most contractors have never calculated that price and so concede it as though it were free.

**Bid-date pricing is treated as the default.** The question "what does this cost" has two different answers depending on whether you mean today or at buyout, and on a metal-heavy scope those answers have diverged by a fifth over the past year.

*On a job at a 6% net margin, that gap is the whole margin. The bet was placed at signing; the result arrives at buyout.*

Run it against your actual numbers. Take the last three contracts you signed. For each: what fraction of contract value is metal-intensive material, what escalation did you carry, and what would the August 2026 category rate have done to it. You are not forecasting. You are checking whether the last twelve months would have fit inside the allowance you wrote.

If the answer is no on two of three, your estimating template has a known defect and it is costing real money on live work.

<h2 id="the-fix">Four mechanisms, in order of how easily you will get them accepted</h2>

1. **Shorten quote validity on metal-intensive scopes.** Thirty days becomes seven or fourteen. This is the cheapest change available and the one owners contest least, because it reads as precision rather than as risk transfer. Say it plainly in the proposal: *pricing for copper and steel packages valid 14 days from the date of this proposal.*

2. **Price volatile commodities at order date, not bid date.** Name the commodity, name the index, name the mechanism. This is standard practice in industrial work and still unusual in commercial and residential, which is exactly why it is available to the contractor who asks first.

3. **Write a real escalation clause — threshold, index, and cap.** A clause that triggers at a defined percentage move in a named published index, with a cap that gives the owner certainty about their worst case. Vague "market conditions" language gets struck. Specific, capped, indexed language survives review far more often because the owner can price it.

4. **Buy out long-lead metal early, or get the hold in writing.** If the supplier will hold price for 60 days, that hold is worth money and should be in the file. If they will not, that refusal is information about where they think prices are going, and it belongs in your bid.

<h2 id="the-forecast">What to assume about next year</h2>

Dodge Construction Network's economists expect materials inflation to peak above 9% around the turn of the year before easing marginally, and have called inflation the defining pressure on 2027. Treat that as a planning assumption rather than a number to bid against — forecasts in this area were revised repeatedly through 2026, in both directions.

The durable point is not the forecast. It is that the gap between a blended index and a metal category has been wide enough, for long enough, that estimating against the blend is now a known error rather than an unlucky one. The contractors who will be fine in 2027 are not the ones who guessed the direction correctly. They are the ones whose contracts do not require them to guess.
