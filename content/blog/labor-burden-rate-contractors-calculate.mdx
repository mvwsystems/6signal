Ask a contractor his labor burden and you will get a round number. Thirty-five percent. Forty. It arrives immediately, with confidence, and it is almost never the output of a calculation anyone has run recently.

Here is the uncomfortable part. The federal government publishes what employers in this industry actually spend, every quarter, for free, and the number is higher than the round one.

<h2 id="the-floor">What does the government say an hour costs?</h2>

The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes Employer Costs for Employee Compensation quarterly. For full-time workers in the construction industry, the June 2026 reference period looks like this:

| Component | Per hour worked |
|---|---|
| Wages and salaries | $36.56 |
| Paid leave | $2.69 |
| Supplemental pay | $2.55 |
| Insurance | $4.00 |
| Retirement and savings | $2.46 |
| Legally required benefits | $4.47 |
| **Total benefits** | **$16.17** |
| **Total compensation** | **$52.73** |

Sixteen dollars and seventeen cents of benefits against thirty-six fifty-six of wages is a **44.2% burden**. That is the industry average across every construction employer in the country, union and open shop, every region, every trade.

And it is a floor, not an answer, because of what it leaves out.

<h2 id="whats-missing">What the 44% does not include</h2>

ECEC measures compensation. It does not measure the cost of putting a person on a job site. Four categories ride on every self-perform hour and appear nowhere in that table:

- **The truck and the fuel.** Lease or depreciation, insurance, fuel, maintenance, tires.
- **The phone or tablet**, and the software seats attached to it.
- **Small tools, consumables, and PPE** — the blades, bits, gloves, and replacements that get expensed to overhead and never traced to an hour.
- **Training, certifications, and safety time** — OSHA 30, confined space, competent person, the toolbox talk, the annual refresher.

None of those is exotic and all of them are real. On a field employee who costs roughly $110,000 a year in wages and benefits, a conservative stack of truck, phone, tools, and training lands somewhere around $11,000 — call it $7,200 for the truck, $900 for the phone and seat, $1,800 for tools and consumables, $1,100 for training and certifications. Your numbers will differ. The category will not.

<h2 id="the-divisor">The divisor is the part nobody argues about and everybody gets wrong</h2>

Here is the move that quietly does the most damage: taking the total annual cost and dividing by 2,080.

A field employee is paid for 2,080 hours. He does not charge 2,080 hours to jobs. Holidays, PTO, training days, weather standby, yard time, shop time, and the morning somebody spent waiting on an inspector all come out of the top. What is left is the only denominator that can actually recover the cost.

The arithmetic, line by line:

| Line | Value |
|---|---|
| Wages — 2,080 hrs × $36.56 | $76,045 |
| Benefits at the BLS rate — 2,080 × $16.17 | $33,634 |
| Truck, fuel, phone, tools, training (not in ECEC) | $11,000 |
| **Total annual cost** | **$120,678** |
| Less PTO and holidays | −120 hrs |
| Less training and certification days | −24 hrs |
| Less weather, standby, shop and yard time | −130 hrs |
| **Hours actually charged to jobs** | **1,806** |
| **True cost per charged hour** | **$66.82** |
| Priced at an assumed 35% burden | $49.36 |
| **Gap** | **$17.46 per charged hour** |

Twelve field employees at 1,800 charged hours each is 21,600 hours. At $17.46 a hour that is roughly **$377,000 a year** — given away silently, on work you won precisely because your number was lower than the number used by the contractor who calculated his.

Every input above is arguable and you should argue with them. Change the vehicle allowance, change the non-productive hours, change the wage to your actual rate, and the answer moves. That is the feature. The useful output of this exercise is not a number to adopt; it is finding out which line you cannot fill in for your own shop.

<h2 id="why-invisible">Why a wrong burden rate never gets caught</h2>

Because the job does not fail.

It comes in at 9% instead of the 14% you bid, and the explanation is always available: weather, a hard inspector, a difficult super, a rough month. Those explanations are plausible and often partly true, which is what makes them so effective at hiding the real cause. One rate, applied identically for six years, is never the suspect, because a suspect requires an event and there was never an event.

The structural version of the problem is the one we keep finding everywhere: **the rate lives in estimating and the actuals live in accounting, and nobody owns the road between them.** The bid gets priced from an assumption. The job gets costed from reality. The two documents describe the same hour and are never compared, so the assumption survives another year and gets repriced into every job you win.

This is exactly what [the margin being gone before the job closed](/research/margin-gone-before-the-job-closed) looks like from the estimating side rather than the field side, and it is the same shape as the [95.5% of construction data that gets captured and never used](/research/construction-data-thrown-away). The information exists. It just never travels.

<h2 id="the-test">The one-employee test</h2>

You do not need a consultant or a software purchase for this. You need one afternoon and a payroll register.

1. **Pick one field employee** — ideally a mid-level, fully typical one, not your best and not your newest.
2. **Add every dollar your company spent on him last year.** Wages. Payroll taxes. Workers' comp at your *current* experience modifier, not last year's. General liability allocation. Health and benefits. Retirement match. PTO and holidays. Training and certifications. Small tools and consumables. Truck, fuel, maintenance, insurance. Phone and software seats.
3. **Pull the hours he actually charged to jobs** from the job-cost system. Not payroll hours. Charged hours.
4. **Divide.** That is what the hour cost you.
5. **Compare it to the rate in your estimating template.** The difference is what you have been giving away, per hour, on every self-perform hour you bid.

If step 3 is the one you cannot complete — if the job-cost system cannot tell you how many hours a specific person charged to jobs last year — stop. That is a bigger finding than the burden rate, and it is the thing to fix first.

<h2 id="the-fix">What to change, and when</h2>

**Recalculate annually, in the month your comp policy renews.** The experience modifier and the comp rate are usually the largest moving part, and recalculating when the new premium lands means the number and its biggest input arrive together.

**Divide by billable hours, always.** If you change nothing else, change the divisor.

**Push last year's actual productivity back into the estimating database.** Not just the rate — the units. The crew that took four days on a two-day phase is information the estimating template has never seen. This is the step that almost never happens, and it is the one that compounds.

**Separate the rate by crew type.** A single company-wide burden hides the same averaging problem that a single company-wide bid hit rate hides. Your concrete crew and your finish carpenters do not carry the same vehicle cost, the same comp class, or the same non-productive ratio.

**Put the recalculation on the calendar as an owner.** Give it a name and a date. Otherwise it is nobody's job, which is how it became a 2019 number in the first place.

<h2 id="the-pattern">The pattern underneath</h2>

Every item above is measurement, not effort. Nobody is being asked to work harder, buy software, or change how they build. The request is that a number which already exists — in the payroll register, in the comp policy, in the job-cost system — arrive somewhere it can be acted on before the next bid goes out.

That is the same argument as [the carrying cost on retainage](/research/slow-payment-is-a-tax-on-your-margin), the same argument as [the rental line nobody reconciled](/research/equipment-and-rental-control-tower), and the same argument as [the company that learns from every job](/research/the-company-that-learns-from-every-job). Discipline is hard to change. Visibility is an engineering problem, and engineering problems have solutions.

If you want the build order rather than the diagnosis, it is in [the first system and the ninety-day order to build it in](/research/the-first-system-90-day-build-order).
