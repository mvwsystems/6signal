For twenty years the question a contractor asked a marketer was some version of *how much traffic did we get*. It was a reasonable question, because traffic and demand moved together. If more people arrived on the site, more people called.

That relationship has been coming apart for a decade, and in 2026 it broke in public.

<h2 id="the-number">How far has it actually moved?</h2>

Rand Fishkin's team at SparkToro ran Similarweb's U.S. desktop and mobile clickstream panel for January through April 2026 and found that 68.01% of Google searches ended without a click to any website. Two years earlier the same measurement produced 60.45%. In 2019 it was around 49%. A decade ago, about 45%.

Two caveats the study states about itself, and they matter. The panel covers browser-based searching only, so Google's mobile app — where zero-click behavior runs higher — is excluded, which makes 68% a floor rather than a ceiling. And paid-click shares were estimated from third-party benchmarks rather than measured directly. Neither undercuts the direction.

Ahrefs, measuring from the other end, tracked more than 75,000 opted-in domains and found Google referral traffic down roughly 8 percentage points — about a 22% relative decline — between June 2025 and May 2026.

<h2 id="not-ai-mode">If it is not AI Mode, what is it?</h2>

Here is the finding most coverage got backwards. Only **0.34%** of searches in that window actually transitioned into AI Mode, Google's dedicated conversational surface. The thing everyone is bracing for has barely started.

The clicks are being absorbed earlier, by the ordinary results page: AI Overviews, expanded panels, People Also Ask, the knowledge graph, and Google's own properties. AI Overviews now appear on a large and growing share of results pages, and when one appears, organic click-through drops by close to 60%.

So the right mental model is not *an AI product is coming for your traffic*. It is *the results page has been quietly becoming the destination for ten years, and generative answers accelerated a trend that was already most of the way there*. AI Mode's query volume is reportedly more than doubling quarter over quarter, which means the second wave is real — it just has not landed yet. Which is the window.

<h2 id="contractor-queries">Does this apply to the searches that actually sell work?</h2>

Less than the headline implies, and that distinction is worth holding onto.

Zero-click rates vary enormously by what the searcher is trying to do. Semrush's 2026 intent data breaks it out:

| Search intent | Zero-click rate | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | 74% | "how long does a roof last" |
| Navigational | 68% | "x-act plumbing phone number" |
| Commercial investigation | 46% | "best slab leak repair company fort worth" |

The queries that matter most to a contractor — somebody actively deciding who to hire — still send a click more than half the time. Homeowners and facility managers do not hire out of a summary paragraph. They hire after they have looked at someone.

Two things follow from that, and they point in opposite directions.

The reassuring one: your commercial queries are the most resilient segment of search, and anybody telling a contractor that SEO is dead is selling something.

The uncomfortable one: *resilient* is not *unchanged*, 46% is already not great, and the informational tier — the one that used to feed the top of your funnel, the "how much does a new HVAC system cost in Dallas" tier — is effectively gone as a traffic channel at 74%. Those answers are still being given. You are just not in the room when they are, unless you were cited.

<h2 id="the-real-shift">What actually changed about the scoreboard</h2>

The traffic number and the visibility number used to be the same number. They are not anymore, and the gap between them is now the most important thing on the report.

We saw this directly in our own tracked data. Across [700 AI visibility checks in one Texas market](/research/we-ran-700-ai-visibility-checks-one-texas-market) — twelve real buyer prompts, five engines, a Maps grid, run over four weeks — the same plumbing company scored 92% mention rate on one engine and zero on another, in the same window, on identical work. No traffic report would have surfaced that. Sessions were flat. The thing that was broken was whether a specific pipeline could see and verify the business, and the only way to find it was to ask the engines directly.

That is the shift. Not *traffic is down*. Traffic was always a proxy, and the proxy has decoupled from the thing it was standing in for. [Ranking is not the same as being recommended](/research/ranking-is-not-the-same-as-being-recommended), and now sessions are not the same as presence.

<h2 id="what-to-do">What do you actually do about it?</h2>

Three moves, in order. None of them is "write more blog posts."

**1. Make sure the engines can reach you at all.** Eligibility comes before everything. If your robots.txt blocks GPTBot, or your service pages render client-side and arrive as an empty shell, or your schema is absent or contradicting your page copy, you are not losing an argument about quality — you were never in the running. That is the Index Engine Optimization layer, and it is the one with a binary answer. [Check whether your site is blocking AI crawlers](/research/is-your-site-blocking-ai-crawlers); it takes about ten minutes and it is the highest-return ten minutes in this document.

**2. Make your entity unambiguous.** Generative systems do not rank you, they resolve you — they assemble a picture of who you are from your site, your Google Business Profile, license registries, directories, and review platforms, and if those sources disagree about your name, your service area, or your trade, confidence drops and the system names somebody it is surer about. That is [the local entity gap](/research/local-entity-gap-ai-search), and it is the most common reason a good company gets skipped. [Fixing the Google Business Profile](/research/how-to-fix-google-business-profile-for-ai-visibility) is where it starts, because every other surface reads from it.

**3. Measure presence directly, on a schedule, with a frozen instrument.** Ask the engines the questions your buyers actually ask — not keywords, questions — record whether you are named, cited, or absent, and run the same list again in two weeks. A single check is an anecdote. A fixed list run repeatedly is a trend line, and a trend line is the only thing that tells you whether a fix worked. The method is in [the AI search measurement playbook](/research/ai-search-measurement-playbook), the honesty rules are in [how to measure AI visibility without lying to yourself](/research/how-to-measure-ai-visibility-without-lying-to-yourself), and [how to read the results](/research/how-to-read-your-ai-visibility-test-results) covers what the bands mean once you have them.

<h2 id="the-window">The argument for doing it now</h2>

Fishkin's conclusion is that there is "not much point of fighting back by simply getting better at SEO," and he is right about the traffic, which is not coming back. But the conclusion contractors should draw is narrower and more useful than *marketing is over*.

Two clocks are running. The engines are improving at answering on a six-month cycle. Local businesses are improving at being findable on a multi-year one — most have not started, and a meaningful share are still actively blocking the crawlers that would index them. The gap between those clocks is not a threat. It is the only reason a $4M contractor can still beat a $40M one to the recommendation.

Zero-click did not remove the opportunity. It moved it from the results page into the answer, where there are three slots instead of ten and almost nobody is competing for them yet.
