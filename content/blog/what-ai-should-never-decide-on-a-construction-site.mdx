# What AI Should Never Decide on a Construction Site

This series has been making the case that AI belongs in the middle of a contracting company — [moving information between the four systems](/research/the-four-system-contractor) instead of letting it die in trucks and inboxes. Before going further into money, safety, and specifications, the boundary needs to be drawn, because everything after depends on it.

Here is the boundary: **AI prepares; humans decide.** It sounds like a slogan. It is actually a scoping rule with teeth, and most of the AI-for-construction pitch deck industry fails it.

<h2 id="the-list">The list</h2>

Decisions that stay human, permanently, regardless of how good the models get:

**On the site:**
- Whether a trench is safe to enter.
- Which protective system is adequate for the soil in front of you.
- Whether atmospheric conditions in a confined space are acceptable.
- Whether fall protection is unnecessary in an edge case.
- Whether lockout/tagout can be bypassed to save an hour.
- Whether damaged rigging or equipment stays in service.
- Whether a worker is qualified or competent for the task.

**In the office:**
- Approving payments.
- Altering the books.
- Determining tax treatment.
- Creating billing that lacks support.
- Certifying that site conditions satisfy a regulation.
- Signing anything.

The site list has a common structure: each is a judgment where the input is physical reality and the cost of error is a life. A model can quote the OSHA trenching standard flawlessly. It cannot see the ground move, smell the gas, or inspect the sling — and it cannot assume the legal role of the competent person, which OSHA assigns to a human being by design. Anyone who tells you their AI "ensures OSHA compliance" is describing a product category that cannot exist.

The office list has a different common structure: each is a point where authority over money or legal exposure changes hands. The moment software exercises that authority autonomously, you have not automated a task — you have created an unaccountable signatory.

<h2 id="what-that-leaves">What that leaves — which is almost everything</h2>

The temptation is to read the list as AI skepticism. Read it again: nothing on it is where contractors actually lose their weeks. The losing happens in the preparation layer — and the preparation layer is enormous:

- Retrieve the current version of the safety procedure, with its revision date.
- Draft the project-specific checklist for a human to edit.
- Translate the approved toolbox talk for a multilingual crew, keeping the English-controlled version.
- Track whose certifications expire next month and say so before mobilization.
- Match the invoice to the PO, the job, and the cost code — and queue the mismatches.
- Flag the duplicate charge, the unbilled change order, the rental that never went off-rent.
- Convert the foreman's voice note into a structured daily report.
- Assemble the exception list a human reviews in ten minutes instead of finding in ninety days.

Every item ends the same way: a prepared decision arriving in front of an accountable person, sooner and with better support than before. That is the whole model. AI compresses the distance between something happening and a human knowing; the human stays where the authority is.

<h2 id="why-the-line-helps-adoption">Why drawing the line early is a competitive act</h2>

There is a practical reason to state the boundary before buying anything, beyond intellectual honesty.

Contractors are not laggards out of stubbornness. In a [CMiC and Dodge survey, 87 percent of contractors said they believe AI will meaningfully impact construction](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/builders-ai-transform-businesses-survey/807555/) — while RICS found only about 12 percent using it regularly. That gap is not ignorance. It is the sound of people who hold real liability declining to hand it to a demo.

A rollout scoped as "the system decides" collides with that instinct and dies in pilot. A rollout scoped as "the system prepares, you decide" removes drudgery without moving accountability — and the same skeptical foreman who killed the last initiative becomes its heaviest user, because it gives him time back and takes nothing from him.

The boundary is not a concession to make AI palatable. It is the design principle that makes it work. The rest of this series — [the money](/research/construction-data-thrown-away), the safety system, the specifications — is built on it, and next week starts where the losses are largest: what it costs a contractor to learn the financial truth ninety days late.
