A $20 million general contractor can tell you, to the dollar, what the steel package cost. The same contractor usually cannot tell you, inside ten minutes, what it cost to wait for money that was already earned.

That second number is larger than most people expect, it recurs every year, and it has never once appeared on a profit and loss statement as itself.

Hold the national total loosely. It rests on a survey of 125 contractors, which is a small sample for a number with a "B" on the end, and every respondent is reporting on themselves. What survives the methodology is the direction and the mechanism: the cost of waiting is real, it is growing, and it is paid in interest and administration rather than in anything anyone would recognize as a loss.

The same report found that 88% of general contractors declined to bid something in the preceding year because of an owner's slow-pay reputation, and that 91% factor payment reputation into their bids at all. That second number is the more interesting one. It means the market has already priced this — just not on your P&L.

<h2 id="your-number">What does it cost your shop, specifically?</h2>

National totals do not change behavior. Four numbers from your own books do.

Take a reference contractor: $20,000,000 in annual volume, 10% retention, held an average of twelve months, borrowing at 9% on the line of credit.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual volume | $20,000,000 |
| Retention rate | 10% |
| Retainage outstanding | $2,000,000 |
| Average days held | 365 |
| Cost of capital (line of credit) | 9.0% |
| **Annual carrying cost** | **$180,000** |
| Net profit at a 3% net margin | $600,000 |
| **Carrying cost as a share of net profit** | **30%** |

Thirty percent of the year's profit, spent on the privilege of waiting for money that was earned and already spent to produce.

Read that against net, not gross, because the gross number flatters everything. CFMA's 2026 Financial Benchmarker — built from 1,359 verified financial statements submitted by contractors and their CPAs, released in September 2026 — puts industry-average net income before taxes at 6.8%, and that blends in specialty trades with much better economics. Pure general contracting and construction management work routinely runs lower. At 3% net, a $20M shop clears about $600,000 for the year, and the retainage carry is a third of it.

Then add the administration. Rabbet's 65 hours a month is 780 hours a year. At a loaded internal rate of $95 an hour — our arithmetic on their figure, not theirs — that is roughly $74,000 of salaried capacity spent moving money that was never in dispute.

<h2 id="why-invisible">Why does this never show up as a problem?</h2>

Three mechanisms, and none of them is carelessness.

**The two halves live in different accounts.** Retainage receivable sits on the balance sheet looking like an asset, because it is one. The interest you pay to survive its absence sits in interest expense, undifferentiated, next to the truck notes. No report connects them. The cost of waiting is never attributed to the thing that caused the wait, so it is never anyone's variance to explain.

**Nothing fails.** There is no bad day, no phone call, no line item. A blown job gets a post-mortem and everyone in the room learns something. A process that quietly costs $180,000 a year and never breaks gets nothing, because [nobody does a post-mortem on a process that never failed loudly](/profit-leak). That is the general shape of every structural leak we have mapped: the information exists, but it lands in the wrong system, on the wrong desk, or three months after the decision it should have informed.

**The third reason is relational, and it is the biggest one.** Everybody in the chain believes that asking hard about retainage costs you the next job. So a measurable cost gets traded for a presumed risk that nobody has ever actually tested. Meanwhile 88% of your peers are declining bids over payment reputation — which means the testing is happening, just in the other direction.

<h2 id="texas">What does Texas law already give you?</h2>

Most general contractors in this state are carrying rights they have never invoked. Three worth knowing, with the standing caveat that this is general information, not legal advice, and that notice requirements decide cases:

**Interest accrues by statute, not by negotiation.** On private projects, Texas Property Code Section 28.004 sets interest on an overdue, undisputed payment at 1.5% per month — 18% a year — starting the day after payment becomes due. Chapter 28 also fixes the clock: an owner generally has 35 days from receipt of an invoice for properly performed work, and a contractor has 7 days from receiving the owner's payment to pay the subs covered by it. One letter citing the statute changes behavior more reliably than five phone calls. Have your attorney confirm the notice requirements before you send it.

**Reserved funds are the owner's obligation, and the vocabulary changed.** Section 53.101 requires an owner to reserve 10% of the contract price during the work and for 30 days after completion. House Bill 2237, effective January 1, 2022 for original contracts signed on or after that date, renamed what used to be called "statutory retainage" to **reserved funds**, and reserved the word *retainage* for the contractual withholding between a contractor and its subs. If your subcontract language still mixes the two terms, it predates the statute.

**Unsigned change orders now have a stopping point.** The 2023 amendments added Section 28.0091 to the Property Code, effective for contracts entered into on or after September 1, 2023. Once the cumulative value of owner-directed additional work without a written, fully executed change order exceeds 10% of the original contract amount, a contractor or subcontractor may decline to proceed with further additional work — and is not responsible for damages associated with that election. That is directly adjacent to the other half of this problem: the [work you did but never billed](/research/work-you-did-but-never-billed).

<h2 id="the-test">The four-number test</h2>

This takes under an hour and requires no software purchase.

1. **Total retainage receivable, right now.** One number out of the accounting system.
2. **Your actual line-of-credit rate.** Not the rate you remember. The one on the last statement.
3. **Multiply, then divide by last year's net profit.** That percentage is the test.
4. **Days held, by owner.** Sort descending. This is the one nobody has, and the one that changes the next go/no-go.

If nobody in your company can produce the third number inside ten minutes, it is not being managed — and it is likely the single largest uncontested expense in the business.

<h2 id="the-fix">What actually fixes it?</h2>

In order of difficulty, cheapest first.

**Compute it.** The carrying cost does not become a managed number until it exists. Put it on the monthly package next to the interest expense it explains.

**Negotiate reduced retention at 50% completion.** It is in more contracts than most general contractors ever invoke, and asking is free.

**Bill retainage on completed, accepted scopes** rather than waiting for full closeout. The sitework sub finished in month three; there is no reason his retention waits for the punch list on a building he never touched.

**Track days-held by owner.** This is the control that compounds. Once you know which owners are genuinely expensive to work for, that number belongs in the go/no-go standard — which is the same place the [cost of losing a pursuit](/profit-leak) belongs, and for the same reason.

**Price a retainage bond.** Typically 1–3% a year of the held amount to release the cash, against the 9% you are borrowing at. Sometimes the arithmetic is obvious and nobody has run it.

**Put the statute in writing.** Not as a threat. As a term, cited once, early, by an attorney who confirmed the notice requirements.

<h2 id="the-system">Why this is a visibility problem, not a discipline problem</h2>

Every item on that list is measurement, not effort. You are not asking anyone to work harder. You are asking a number that already exists — in the accounting system, on the bank statement, in the aging report — to arrive somewhere it can be acted on.

That is the whole argument, and it is the same argument we make about [the burden rate nobody recalculated](/research/labor-burden-rate-contractors-calculate), about [weekly estimate-versus-actual](/research/margin-gone-before-the-job-closed), about [the financial pipeline from field to estimate](/research/from-bookkeeping-to-financial-intelligence), and about [the 95.5% of construction data that gets captured and never used](/research/construction-data-thrown-away). Contractors who lose money to structural leaks are not undisciplined. Most of them are the hardest-working people in any room they walk into. They are operating a business whose information moves slower than its decisions, and discipline cannot close that gap. Only a system can.

The practical version is boring and it works: a standing monthly number for retainage carry, a days-held column sorted descending, a certificate expiration list, and a reason-for-lost-time field on the daily report. Four small pipes. We put the build order for that kind of work in [the first system, and the ninety-day order to build it in](/research/the-first-system-90-day-build-order), and the broader architecture in [the connective layer](/research/the-connective-layer-ai-infrastructure-construction).

Your P&L will tell you that you lost money. It will never tell you where. That part you have to build.
