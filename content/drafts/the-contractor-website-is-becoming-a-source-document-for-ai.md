Ask what a contractor website is for, and you'll get the 2015 answer: look professional, show the work, get the call. That answer assumed every visitor was a human with eyes. The assumption quietly broke.

## Short answer

A growing share of your website's most consequential "readers" are retrieval systems assembling answers — ChatGPT grounding a recommendation, Google composing an AI Overview, Perplexity choosing what to cite. These readers don't experience your design, your hero video, or your slogan. They extract facts: what you do, where, for whom, at what cost, with what proof. That makes the website's emerging job **source document** — the primary first-party record machines quote when deciding whether you belong in an answer. Most contractor sites were built as brochures: persuasive to humans, nearly empty to machines. The fix isn't a redesign. It's making every important page state extractable, verifiable, specific facts — while staying persuasive to the human who arrives after the machine's answer sent them.

## The two readers

Every page you publish now serves two audiences with different needs:

**The human buyer** skims, feels, and judges: does this company look legitimate, do I understand what they do, can I call easily? Design, photography, and tone all work on this reader.

**The machine reader** parses. It wants declarative statements it can lift and check: *"X-Act Plumbing repairs slab leaks in Red Oak, Waxahachie, and Ferris. Licensed Master Plumber RMP-44569. Same-day emergency response."* Three sentences, five verifiable claims, all extractable. The machine cannot use "quality craftsmanship you can trust" for anything — there's no fact in it.

Here's the part that keeps this from being a trade-off: the second reader's requirements make pages *better* for the first. Buyers also want the facts fast. The brochure era optimized for a vague impression; the source-document era optimizes for stated substance — and humans reward that too.

## What "source document" means in practice

A source document earns its name by passing the tests an engine implicitly applies before quoting anything:

1. **It answers a question someone actually asks.** Each money page maps to real buyer language — the phrasing from your calls and reviews, not the phrasing from your industry.
2. **The answer is at the top.** What the service covers, where, who it's for, what drives the cost, how fast you respond — visible, textual, before the scroll. This is the [answer-ready service page](/research/how-to-build-an-answer-ready-service-page) standard.
3. **Its claims are checkable.** License numbers in text. Real towns. Honest ranges. Specifics that agree with your profile, your directories, and your reviews — because engines cross-reference, and contradiction reads as risk.
4. **It's structurally legible.** Headings that mean something, lists where lists belong, one page per service instead of nineteen services in one paragraph. And markup that translates the visible content — [schema as a translation layer](/research/schema-is-not-a-hack-translation-layer), never a costume.
5. **Nothing important is locked away.** Substance in PDFs, behind forms, or inside images is substance no engine will ever quote. If it matters, it lives in open HTML.

## Why this is an advantage for contractors specifically

Contractors hold an unfair advantage in the source-document era that most will never use: **you know things the internet doesn't.** What a slab leak actually costs in your county. Which panel brands fail. What insurance adjusters accept. Why the cheap bid is cheap. Machines assembling answers are starving for exactly this — grounded, specific, locally true material — and mostly finding national filler and directory boilerplate instead.

The contractor who writes down what they already know, in plain declarative pages organized around [the questions buyers actually ask](/research/question-form-search-aeo-opportunity), becomes the best source material in their market more or less by default. Not because of marketing skill — because of scarcity. Real expertise, stated plainly, is the rarest content format online.

## The brochure-site failure modes

What we find in audits, in descending order of damage:

- **Superlative pages.** "Unmatched service, unbeatable prices" — zero extractable facts, and unverifiable claims lower a page's usefulness to a cautious engine.
- **The everything page.** One "Services" page listing nineteen trades in a wall of text. Machines match pages to questions; a page about everything matches nothing.
- **The missing where.** Sites that never plainly state the service area, leaving the single most important fact for local retrieval to inference.
- **Trapped proof.** The license number in a footer image, the testimonials in a slider that renders as JavaScript soup, the project details in a gallery with no words.
- **Facts that disagree.** The site says one thing, the profile another, the directories a third — and the machine trusts none of them. That's the [local entity gap](/research/local-entity-gap-ai-search) wearing a web design.

## What to do about it

Don't rebuild the site. Re-job it, one page at a time, starting with the page that corresponds to the work you most want booked:

1. Rewrite the top of that page to state, in visible text: what, where, for whom, cost drivers, response time, and proof.
2. Add the four or five real questions buyers ask about that service, answered substantively.
3. Check every claim against your profile and directories; fix the disagreements.
4. Mark it up truthfully, link it to the towns it serves, and move to the next page.

That's an afternoon per page, and each page converts twice — once for the machine deciding whether to name you, once for the human who arrives already half-convinced because an answer sent them.
