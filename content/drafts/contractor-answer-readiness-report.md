## Executive summary

For twenty years, contractor marketing had one scoreboard: rankings. Rank for "roofer + city," win the click, win the call. That scoreboard still exists — but a second one has appeared above it, and most of the trades haven't noticed. When a homeowner asks ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, or Google's AI features *"who should I call about a slab leak,"* *"is this roof damage worth an insurance claim,"* or *"best electrician near me for a panel upgrade,"* the machine doesn't return a ranked list. It returns an **answer** — and either your company is usable inside that answer, or it isn't.

**Answer-readiness** is the property of being usable: the engine can identify you (entity clarity), verify you (proof), match you to the problem (service and question coverage), and hand you to the buyer (conversion path). This report defines the standard, explains why contractors are the most exposed category on this surface, breaks the requirement down trade by trade — from roofers to commercial GCs — and provides the five working models (service page, review, GBP, question cluster, schema/source) plus a scorecard and a 30/60/90 plan.

The core finding from our field work is uncomfortable but consistent: **the contractors being named by answer engines are frequently not the best contractors in their market — they're the most legible ones.** Craft quality doesn't transmit through a retrieval pipeline. Records do. The good news is symmetrical: legibility is buildable, most competitors haven't built it, and the trades' deep well of real expertise is exactly the raw material answer engines are starving for.

## Short answer

A contractor is answer-ready when an AI engine, asked a real buyer question in their market, can do four things without guessing: **identify** the business (consistent name, categories, services, service area across site, Google Business Profile, and directories), **verify** it (recent, specific reviews and third-party corroboration), **match** it to the question (service pages and question content that actually address what buyers ask), and **route** the buyer (a working phone/booking path). Most contractors fail at step one — not because they're bad businesses, but because their public records are thin, stale, or contradictory. Answer-readiness is built in layers, in order, and measured against real prompts on real engines — never assumed.

## Part 1: Why contractors are uniquely exposed

Four properties make the trades the highest-stakes category in AI search:

1. **The queries are compressible.** "Who's a good plumber near me" collapses perfectly into a two-name answer. Nobody asks for ten options when water is on the floor.
2. **The stakes push engines toward conservatism.** Recommending a bad taco stand costs a bad lunch; recommending a bad foundation company costs tens of thousands. Engines lean harder on verifiable proof before naming trade businesses — which punishes thin records disproportionately.
3. **The category's records are historically bad.** Decade-old directory listings, DIY websites, unclaimed profiles, review responses that stopped in 2021. The average contractor's public data would embarrass a lemonade stand — and it's what the machine reads.
4. **The buyer's questions are answerable.** Trades run on concrete questions with real answers: costs, warning signs, repair-versus-replace, code requirements, insurance mechanics. This is exactly the content answer engines reward — and almost no contractor publishes it, which is why directories and national brands fill the vacuum. ([Why Directories and Aggregators Keep Winning AI Answers](/research/why-directories-and-aggregators-keep-winning-ai-answers) documents the pattern.)

## Part 2: Emergency vs. high-consideration trades

Answer-readiness is one standard with two operating modes, and knowing your mode sets your priorities.

**Emergency mode** (burst pipes, dead AC in July, stuck garage door, no power): the buyer asks one question, takes the first credible answer, and calls. The answer window is minutes. What wins: hours accuracy, review recency, response-speed evidence, emergency-specific content ("what to do before the plumber arrives"), and a frictionless call path. Being second in the answer is close to worthless.

**High-consideration mode** (roof replacement, remodel, foundation repair, commercial builds): the buyer asks dozens of questions across weeks — diagnosis, cost, process, trust — and verifies everything. What wins: question-cluster depth, review *specificity*, corroboration across third-party sources, and content that survives a skeptical re-read. Being present across the buyer's whole question journey beats being first in any single answer.

Most trades run both modes (a plumber does emergency calls and repipes; an electrician does no-power calls and panel upgrades). Build for your dominant mode first, but cover both.

## Part 3: What answer engines need before naming a business

Strip away the vendor mystique and the requirement reduces to four layers:

- **Identity** — one unambiguous entity: same name, same services, same territory everywhere the machine looks. Contradiction reads as risk; risk gets skipped. (The gap between records is the [local entity gap](/research/the-local-entity-gap-in-contractor-marketing).)
- **Evidence** — reviews with content (service + place + outcome, not "great job!"), ratings competitive for the market, recency proving the business is alive, and third-party pages that agree.
- **Match** — content that addresses the actual question asked. An engine answering "tank vs tankless for a family of four" cannot use a page that says "we install water heaters. Call today."
- **Route** — a working way to act: correct phone, honest hours, a booking path that functions on a phone at night.

Everything in this report is one of those four layers wearing work clothes.

## Part 4: Trade-by-trade

The standard is universal. The buyer questions, proof burdens, and content priorities are not.

**Roofers.** High-consideration, storm-driven, insurance-mediated. The question set that decides you: hail damage recognition, claim timelines, repair vs. replace at year 15, what inspections check, deductible legality. Proof burden: heavy — storm-chaser scars make buyers and engines skeptical; local longevity, address-stable reviews, and insurance-process fluency are the differentiators. Trap: chasing "roofing company + city" while the buyer's real query is "is my roof damage worth a claim."

**Plumbers.** Dual-mode. Emergency: slab leaks, burst pipes, sewage backup — hours, recency, speed. Consideration: repipes, water heaters, remodels — cost honesty and comparison content ("tank vs tankless" done with real math). Reviews naming the *specific* fix and town are disproportionately valuable because plumbing queries are problem-specific.

**HVAC.** Seasonal emergency spikes plus a heavy repair-vs-replace decision layer. Winning content: honest replace math at year 12+, why one room won't cool, what a tune-up actually includes, refrigerant regulation changes in plain English. Maintenance-plan clarity is both a conversion asset and answer material.

**Electricians.** The safety-anxiety trade. Buyers ask fear questions — federal-pacific panels, aluminum wiring, breaker trips — and modernization questions (EV chargers, panel upgrades, generators). License visibility matters more here than any trade except foundation; put the license number in text on the site, the profile, and the pages.

**Remodelers.** Pure high-consideration. The answer engines' buyer asks process and trust questions: real cost ranges by project tier, timeline honesty, permit handling, how payment schedules should work, what a red-flag contract looks like. Portfolio pages with specifics (scope, duration, town) function as proof documents; galleries without words are invisible to the surface.

**Garage doors.** Emergency-heavy, commodity-priced, scam-scarred — the category's bad actors did contractors a favor: buyers ask "fair price for spring replacement" and "how do I avoid garage door scams," and an honest local company that answers those questions plainly is positioned to own the trust query the whole category triggers.

**Landscaping.** Recurring-service mode. Buyers compare on reliability, plans, and price structure. Coverage breadth wins: town-tagged reviews and per-town service clarity across the whole territory. Seasonal question content (when to plant/treat/prune in your region) builds the recurring-authority profile engines associate with the entity.

**Tree service.** Emergency (storm damage) plus risk (insurance, liability, "is this tree dangerous"). Proof of insurance and credentials (ISA certification if held) belong in text, not just badges. Storm-response content written *before* storm season is the classic preparation trade: the questions are predictable annually.

**Pest control.** Recurring plus urgent-disgust queries ("found termite wings," "roaches after treatment?"). Treatment-process explainers and honest guarantee terms are the extractable content. Plan pricing clarity separates you from the national brands' quote-wall — and engines notice what buyers can actually read.

**Foundation/concrete.** The highest skepticism trade — buyers actively ask "how do I know I'm not being scammed." Which cracks matter, piers vs. slab jacking, warranty transferability, engineer-report mechanics: a company that answers these honestly (including "when you *don't* need us") becomes the trusted source in a category defined by distrust. Longest consideration cycle; deepest cluster wins.

**Commercial contractors/GCs.** Different buyer (facility managers, owners' reps, developers), same mechanics, plus Copilot exposure via enterprise tooling. Answer material: bonding capacity, delivery methods (design-build vs. bid-build in plain terms), sector experience with named project types, safety record. The proof layer shifts from consumer reviews to project corroboration — trade press, permits, association records.

## Part 5: The five models

**The answer-ready service page.** One page per money service. Above the fold, in visible text: what it covers, where, for whom, what drives cost, how fast you respond, why you're credible. Then depth: process, real ranges, four or five substantial Q&As. Written in the buyer's phrasing. Template: [How to Build an Answer-Ready Service Page](/research/how-to-build-an-answer-ready-service-page).

**The review specificity model.** Volume and recency get you counted; *content* gets you matched. The ask that changes everything: "If you don't mind, mention what we fixed and which town you're in." A review base that collectively names your services and towns is a corpus of third-party evidence engines can mine. Details: [What Contractor Reviews Need to Say for AI Visibility](/research/what-contractor-reviews-need-to-say-for-ai-visibility).

**The GBP alignment model.** The profile is your entity hub — Google's guidance says to keep it updated, names relevance/distance/prominence as the local factors, and reminds everyone there's no paying for position. The alignment rule: profile, site, and directories tell one identical story — categories, services, area, phone, hours. Full treatment: [Google Business Profile Is Becoming Local AI Infrastructure](/research/google-business-profile-local-ai-infrastructure).

**The question cluster model.** For each money service: the 8–12 questions buyers ask in the two weeks before hiring, each answered substantively and locally, interlinked with the parent page. Emergency/decision questions first, cost second, diagnostics third. Architecture: [Question Clusters: The Content Architecture of AEO](/research/question-clusters-content-architecture-of-aeo).

**The schema/source model.** LocalBusiness subtype site-wide; Service + FAQPage markup matching visible text (Google's stated rule); and the corroboration footprint — the 8–10 directories engines actually cite in your market, made consistent. Schema translates; sources corroborate; neither substitutes for content. Argument: [Schema Is Not a Hack](/research/schema-is-not-a-hack-translation-layer).

## Part 6: The Contractor Answer-Readiness Scorecard

Score each dimension 0–4 (0 = absent, 2 = exists but generic/stale, 4 = complete, specific, current). Under 20 total: you're invisible to the surface. 20–29: present but losing contested answers. 30+: competitive, maintain and expand.

| Dimension | What 4/4 looks like |
|---|---|
| Entity consistency | Site, GBP, top directories identical on name/services/area/phone |
| GBP completeness | Right categories, all services, true area, photos, seeded Q&A |
| Review evidence | Recent, specific (service+town), responded-to, competitive volume |
| Service page depth | Money services each have a true source document |
| Question coverage | 8+ real buyer questions answered substantively |
| Local coverage | Every town served has dedicated presence and proof |
| Technical access | Crawlable, parseable, schema valid and truthful |
| Conversion path | Tap-to-call, honest hours, working mobile booking |
| Verification | Real prompts tested on real engines, logged, on a cadence |
| Proof of movement | ✗→✓ flips recorded across cycles |

## Part 7: The 30/60/90 plan

**Days 1–30 — identify and stabilize.** Baseline prompt testing across five engines ([the measurement playbook](/research/ai-search-measurement-playbook)); GBP corrected and completed; review engine installed; top directories reconciled. Exit: one entity, one story, proof accumulating.

**Days 31–60 — match.** Top two service pages rebuilt as source documents; first question cluster (8–12 questions) live around the #1 service; town pages for the core territory. Exit: the questions that precede your best jobs have real answers on your domain.

**Days 61–90 — translate and verify.** Schema deployed and validated; internal links tightened; conversion path tested on a phone at night; full prompt-set retest against the day-1 baseline. Exit: a before/after table and cycle two's priorities, chosen by the gaps — not by mood.

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming craft transmits.** "Our work speaks for itself" — it doesn't; records speak. The best roofer in the county is routinely out-answered by the third-best with better legibility.
2. **Brochure pages.** Superlatives where facts should be. Engines can't extract "unmatched quality."
3. **Generic review begging.** Volume without content builds a number, not evidence.
4. **Keyword-stuffed profile names** ("X-Act Plumbing | Best Plumber Red Oak 24/7") — violates Google's representation guidelines and reads as spam to everyone, machine and human.
5. **One "Services" page for nineteen services.** Machines match pages to questions; a page about everything matches nothing.
6. **Skipping verification.** Shipping all of it and never asking a single engine a single buyer question.
7. **Quitting at day 45.** The trades' advantage is that competitors quit. Compounding pays the persistent.

## What to fix first

One afternoon: run fifteen real buyer prompts across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google, and Maps, and log who gets named. One week: make your GBP agree with your website exactly, and install the review ask. One month: rebuild your single most valuable service page as a source document. That sequence — measure, stabilize, match — outperforms any amount of random effort.

## What this means for operators

You already possess the scarce asset: real answers to real questions, earned in crawlspaces and on rooftops. The machines assembling your market's answers are starving for exactly that expertise in extractable form. Answer-readiness is the discipline of writing down what you already know, in the places machines read, consistently. It is not marketing cleverness. It's legibility — and right now, in most markets, legibility is nearly uncontested.

## Limitations and caveats

No practice guarantees being named — engines change, markets differ, and Google explicitly offers no purchasable position in local results or AI features. Timelines vary with market density: solo-operator markets can move in one cycle; saturated metros take two or three. Some answer variance is irreducible; the goal is raising your rate across prompts and engines, not owning every answer every time. And the evidence base here is Google's published guidance plus our own logged client testing — where we state a pattern (per-engine divergence, entity-fix response times), it's first-party field observation, not a controlled study.
